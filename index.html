BMW Is Powerful, Beemer Is Not: Nickname Branding Impairs Brand Performance
JM Zhang, Ye & Thomson (2025) · Journal of Marketing, 89(1), 135-152
ExperimentsSecondary DataField Study
ABSTRACT
This research investigates nickname branding, a novel phenomenon whereby firms incorporate the "street" names consumers give brands into their own marketing (e.g., Bloomingdale's opening a "Bloomie's" store). While practitioners anticipate positive results from deploying this tactic, the current research serves as the first empirical investigation of its likely effectiveness. Drawing on speech act theory, the authors theorize that using a nickname in place of a formal name serves as an act of power redistribution, effectively signaling submission to consumers, thereby reducing the perception of a brand's power and weakening its performance. Through a multimethod approach that incorporates secondary data analyses, field studies, and preregistered experiments, the results support this view across a range of performance metrics.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Based on speech act theory (illocutionary meaning in marketing). The authors argue that a brand nickname is not merely an informal label but a product of social negotiations that signifies power dynamics in the brand-consumer relationship. When a brand adopts a consumer-created nickname, it performs an act of power redistribution -- effectively signaling submission to consumers. This is the first application of speech act theory to brand naming strategy, revealing that brand nicknames carry important social and political implications beyond their surface-level informality. The framework predicts that this power shift will weaken brand performance across multiple dimensions including status perception, pricing power, and consumer engagement.
RESEARCH VARIABLES
IV
Nickname branding (use of consumer-given nickname vs. formal brand name)
DV
Brand performance: brand status, price premium, consumer engagement
Mediator
Perceived brand power (power redistribution)
Context
Multiple brand categories (BMW/Beemer, Bloomingdale's/Bloomie's)
RESEARCH METHOD
Multimethod approach combining three types of evidence: (1) Secondary data analyses of historical social media data examining real-world brand nickname usage and its performance correlates; (2) Field studies testing nickname branding effects with actual consumers in natural settings; (3) Multiple preregistered controlled experiments manipulating whether participants see a brand's social media post using the formal name versus the consumer nickname. The multimethod design ensures convergent validity across different contexts and measurement approaches.
CORE FINDINGS
Nickname branding consistently impairs brand performance across all studies and metrics. Using informal consumer-given names in official brand communications: (1) damages perceived brand status by signaling that the brand has ceded power to consumers; (2) reduces consumers' willingness to pay price premiums; (3) lowers consumer engagement metrics. The mechanism is rooted in power perception -- consumers interpret nickname adoption as the brand "lowering itself," which paradoxically reduces rather than enhances the brand's appeal.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION
First empirical investigation of nickname branding effectiveness. Counterintuitive finding: practitioners assume incorporating consumer nicknames increases relatability, but the research demonstrates the opposite. Introduces speech act theory to branding research. Actionable guidance for brand managers considering whether to adopt consumer-originated brand nicknames.

A2
When Diversity Backfires: The Asymmetric Role of Multicultural Diversity on Brand Perception
JCR Uduehi, Saint Clair, Hamilton & Reed (2025) · Journal of Consumer Research, 52(2), 330-350
6 Experiments
ABSTRACT
Brands are expanding their market to new consumers by displaying cultural diversity in marketing campaigns -- but the risks are unclear. This article explores the role of the brand's characteristics in multicultural diversity. Our findings suggest that when brands launch a new multicultural diversity campaign, it may be well-received if the brand's original focus was on the needs of nonmarginalized consumers. However, for brands with a focus on the needs of marginalized consumers (i.e., marginalized-focused brands), such a campaign may be perceived as selling out, thereby negatively impacting consumers' reactions. Six studies with Black and White U.S. consumers explore this novel "sellout effect" across various product categories and for consequential behaviors. Inclusion is identified as a key mechanism, mitigation strategies are explored, and implications for theory and practice are discussed.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Proposes the novel "sellout effect" framework. Integrates identity signaling theory with brand positioning literature. Inclusion is the key mediating mechanism. Develops a typology of four CDI (Customer Diversity Initiative) types based on: customer perception (outgroup vs. dissociative group) and duration (episodic vs. ongoing). When marginalized-focused brands pursue diversity, their core consumers feel less included rather than more, triggering identity signaling threat and perceived betrayal.
RESEARCH VARIABLES
IV
Brand type (marginalized-focused vs. nonmarginalized-focused) x Diversity campaign
DV
Consumer reactions, brand perception, consequential behaviors
Mediator
Perceived inclusion
Moderators
Consumer race, product category, CDI type
RESEARCH METHOD
Six experimental studies with Black and White U.S. consumers across various product categories. Progressive design: early studies establish the basic sellout effect, middle studies test the inclusion mediation mechanism, later studies examine boundary conditions and mitigation strategies. Uses consequential dependent measures (not just attitudes but behavioral intentions).
CORE FINDINGS
Marginalized-focused brands launching multicultural campaigns face backlash ("selling out") from core consumers; non-marginalized brands benefit from same campaigns. Inclusion perceptions mediate. Ongoing dissociative CDIs trigger strongest backlash via identity signaling threat and perceived betrayal. Aligning CDI duration with relationship types mitigates negative reactions for sincere brands.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION
First paper identifying asymmetric effect of multicultural diversity based on original brand positioning. Introduces "sellout effect" as new construct. Challenges the assumption that diversity is universally positive. Four-type CDI typology provides structured framework for managers.

When Learning Negative Brand Associations Leads to Positive Evaluations of Effectiveness
JCR Saint Clair & Cunha (2024) · Journal of Consumer Research, 51(3), 497-519
4 ExperimentsField Study
ABSTRACT
Combining associative learning and goal pursuit literatures, this research shows that the influence of affect transfer on brands depends on the active mindset at the time of judgment. Negatively-valenced brand pairings may become desirable when consumers have an instrumentality mindset. Four experiments and one field study across health, entertainment, and news contexts demonstrate that negative associations can paradoxically lead to positive evaluations of effectiveness via a bidirectional association between unpleasantness and instrumentality.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Integrates associative learning theory with goal pursuit theory. Proposes bidirectional association between unpleasantness and instrumentality. When consumers are in an instrumentality mindset, negative affect signals that a product "works" (like bad-tasting medicine). This challenges the traditional assumption that negative associations always harm brands.
RESEARCH VARIABLES
IV
Brand association valence (negative vs. positive)
DV
Evaluations of effectiveness, purchase intention
Mediator
Bidirectional unpleasantness-instrumentality association
Moderator
Active mindset (instrumentality vs. hedonic)
RESEARCH METHOD
Four controlled experiments plus one field study spanning health (medicine), entertainment (news), and consumer product contexts. Each study manipulates the valence of brand associations while measuring or manipulating the active consumer mindset.
CORE FINDINGS
Negative brand associations can paradoxically enhance perceived effectiveness. The mechanism is a bidirectional association: unpleasantness signals instrumentality. The effect is robust across health, entertainment, and news contexts. The instrumentality mindset is the key moderator activating this paradoxical effect.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION
Counterintuitive finding that negative associations can enhance brand perceptions under specific conditions. Bridges two previously separate theoretical traditions (associative learning and goal pursuit). Novel practical implications for brands in instrumental categories (health, productivity).

They're Not My People: When Inclusive Marketing Backfires
JAMS Hassan, McGowan & Shiu (2025) · JAMS, 53(2), 563-587
10 Experiments
ABSTRACT
This research investigates when customer diversity initiatives (CDIs) backfire and proposes strategies to mitigate this. CDIs targeting a dissociative group more permanently evoke higher levels of brand distancing behaviors among existing customers, driven by identity signaling threat and perceived betrayal. Ten experimental studies distinguish between four types of brand initiatives based on customer perceptions and duration.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Identity signaling theory. Four-type CDI typology based on customer perception (outgroup vs. dissociative group) and duration (episodic vs. ongoing). The most damaging combination is ongoing CDIs targeting dissociative groups, because these permanently threaten existing customers' ability to use the brand as an identity signal.
RESEARCH VARIABLES
IV
CDI type (4 categories based on group x duration)
DV
Brand distancing behaviors
Mediators
Identity signaling threat, perceived betrayal
Moderator
Brand sincerity, sub-brand strategy
RESEARCH METHOD
Ten experimental studies -- one of the most rigorous multi-study designs in recent JAMS publications. Studies progressively narrow the conditions under which CDI backlash occurs and test mitigation strategies including sub-brand/product customization to reduce identity signaling threat.
CORE FINDINGS
Ongoing dissociative CDIs trigger the strongest backlash. Identity signaling threat and perceived betrayal are the dual mediators. For sincere brands, aligning CDI duration with relationship types mitigates negative reactions. Sub-brand and product customization strategies reduce identity threat.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION
10-study design provides exceptional rigor. Identifies specific conditions under which inclusion initiatives backfire. Provides actionable mitigation strategies. Advances the diversity and inclusion conversation with nuanced rather than blanket recommendations.

Avengers, Assemble! Network-Based Contingency Analysis of Spillover Effects in Multi-Brand Alliances
JAMS Klostermann, Hinze, Volckner, Kupfer & Schwerdtfeger (2024) · JAMS, 52(2), 449-469
Network AnalysisSecondary Data
ABSTRACT
Brand alliances are becoming increasingly complex, as marketers have begun to combine not only two but multiple brands to foster spillover effects. A particularly complex brand-alliance strategy is team brands, which combine various brands under a team-brand name. Using data from the Marvel brand universe, we examine contingency factors of sales spillover effects between team brands (e.g., Avengers) and their constituent brands (e.g., Hulk). We investigate the moderating role of key network characteristics from both a firm perspective (brand-brand networks) and a consumer perspective (brand-association networks).
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Network-based contingency framework examining multi-brand alliances through dual network lenses. Firm-side brand-brand network captures strategic combination decisions. Consumer-side brand-association network captures mental associations. RESEARCH VARIABLES
IV
Multi-brand alliance composition (team brands & constituent brands)
DV
Sales spillover effects (bidirectional)
Moderators
Firm-side network properties, consumer-side network properties, spillover direction
Context
Marvel brand universe
RESEARCH METHOD
Network analysis combining firm-side and consumer-side perspectives using the Marvel brand universe as empirical context. Team brands (Avengers, X-Men) and constituent brands (Hulk, Iron Man) form a rich multi-brand alliance network. Sales data captures actual spillover effects. Dual-network approach tests whether firm and consumer perspectives yield different moderating effects.
CORE FINDINGS
Network characteristics strongly moderate spillovers, but their impact depends on both the direction (constituent-to-team vs. team-to-constituent) and the network type (brand-brand vs. brand-association). Central brands generate stronger spillovers, but patterns differ across network types.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION

Brand Extension or Co-Branding: The Consumer's Attribution of Responsibility to Heritage Brands
JRCS Zhang, Guo & Qu (2025) · JRCS, 82, 104133
4 ExperimentsCo-branding
ABSTRACT
Cross-over brand extension and co-branding are effective innovation strategies for heritage brands to expand into new markets. This research compares consumers' responses to these two strategies. Based on the conceptual link between responsibility attribution and authenticity, across four experimental studies, this research uncovers the advantage of brand extension over co-branding and its underlying mechanism: the serial mediation of responsibility attribution and new product authenticity. Meanwhile, the consumer's mindsets (fixed vs. growth) are identified as a boundary condition. Compared to brand extension, consumers attribute less responsibility to the heritage brand in co-branding, weakening perceived new product authenticity and leading to lower purchase intention. The growth mindset enhances authenticity perception in co-branding, making the advantage of brand extension disappear.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Responsibility attribution theory linked to product authenticity perception. Serial mediation: crossover strategy type -> responsibility attribution -> perceived new product authenticity -> purchase intention. Growth mindset boundary condition suggests consumers who view heritage brands as capable of evolving may attenuate the negative co-branding effect.
RESEARCH VARIABLES
IV
Crossover strategy type (brand extension vs. co-branding)
DV
Purchase intention, consumer evaluations
Serial Mediators
Responsibility attribution -> New product authenticity
Moderator
Consumer mindset (fixed vs. growth)
RESEARCH METHOD
Four experimental studies comparing brand extension vs. co-branding for heritage brands. Progressive design testing basic effect, serial mediation, moderating role of consumer mindset, and boundary conditions.
CORE FINDINGS
Brand extension outperforms co-branding for heritage brands. Serial mediation: co-branding -> lower responsibility attribution -> weaker authenticity perception -> lower purchase intention. Growth mindset moderates: eliminates the advantage of brand extension by enhancing co-branding authenticity perception.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION
Directly relevant to our co-branding research. Novel comparison via responsibility attribution mechanism. Serial mediation model is methodologically clean. Growth mindset boundary condition opens consumer-level moderator possibilities.

Revenue Generation Through Influencer Marketing
JM Beichert, Bayerl, Goldenberg & Lanz (2024) · Journal of Marketing, 88(4), 40-63
1.88M TransactionsField StudySecondary Data
ABSTRACT
Direct-to-consumer (DTC) firms increasingly believe that influencer marketing is an effective option for seeding. However, the current managerially relevant question for DTC firms of whether to target low- or high-followership influencers to generate immediate revenue is still unresolved. In this article, the authors' goal is to answer this question by considering for the first time the whole influencer-marketing funnel, i.e., from followers on user-generated content networks, to reached followers, to engagement, to actual revenue, while accounting for the cost of paid endorsements. The authors find that low-followership targeting outperforms high-followership targeting by order of magnitude across three performance (ROI) metrics. A mediation analysis reveals that engagement can explain the negative relationship between the influencer followership levels and return on investment.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Full-funnel influencer marketing model integrating all stages: followers -> reach -> engagement -> revenue, while accounting for costs. Unlike prior research examining individual stages in isolation, this framework treats the entire pipeline as an interconnected system where bottlenecks at any stage affect overall ROI.
RESEARCH VARIABLES
IV
Influencer followership level (low vs. high)
DV
Revenue and three ROI metrics
Mediator
Engagement rate
Data
1.88M purchases (>17M EUR), 319 paid influencers
RESEARCH METHOD
Large-scale secondary sales data from a major DTC e-commerce brand: 1.88 million purchase transactions (>17M EUR) linked to influencer-specific Instagram discount codes, enabling direct attribution. Supplemented by field studies with 319 paid influencers for causal inference. Mediation analysis on the full funnel.
CORE FINDINGS
Low-followership (nano/micro) targeting outperforms by an order of magnitude across all three ROI metrics. Engagement mediates the negative followership-ROI relationship. Nano-influencers' communication styles align more closely with followers' expectations. Cost efficiency dramatically favors nano/micro when accounting for endorsement fees.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION
First study covering the complete influencer funnel to revenue. Dataset (1.88M transactions) among largest in the field. Provides direct ROI evidence rather than engagement proxies. Exemplary secondary data paper demonstrating how transaction data + tracking codes create natural experimental setups.

Influencer Marketing Effectiveness: A Meta-Analytic Review
JAMS Pan, Blut, Ghiassaleh & Lee (2025) · JAMS, 53(1), 52-78
Meta-Analysis251 Papers1531 Effects
ABSTRACT
This meta-analysis synthesizes 1,531 effect sizes from 251 papers to assess influencer marketing effectiveness by examining its antecedents, mediators, and moderators. Building on the persuasion knowledge model, we identify post, follower, and influencer characteristics as key antecedents impacting both non-transactional (attitude, behavioral engagement, purchase intention) and transactional (purchase behavior, sales) marketing outcomes. For non-transactional outcomes, follower characteristics (social identity) have the strongest effects. For transactional outcomes, influencer characteristics (communication) have the strongest effects.
THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK
Persuasion Knowledge Model as organizing framework. Distinguishes non-transactional outcomes (attitude, engagement, intention) from transactional outcomes (purchase, sales). Social media types and product types as contextual moderators.
RESEARCH VARIABLES
Antecedents
Post characteristics, follower characteristics, influencer characteristics
DVs
Non-transactional (attitude, engagement, intention) + Transactional (purchase, sales)
Mediators
Persuasion knowledge, source credibility
Moderators
Social media type, product type
RESEARCH METHOD
Meta-analysis of 1,531 effect sizes from 251 papers. Systematic quantitative synthesis with moderator analysis. Distinguishes between outcome types for nuanced conclusions.
CORE FINDINGS
Follower social identity -> strongest for attitudes/engagement. Post informational/hedonic value -> strongest for purchase intention. Influencer communication -> strongest for actual purchase behavior. Platform type and product type moderate all relationships.
INNOVATION & CONTRIBUTION
Most comprehensive influencer meta-analysis (251 papers, 1531 effects). Key innovation: distinguishing transactional vs. non-transactional outcomes reveals different antecedents matter for different outcomes.
